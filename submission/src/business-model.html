01

The thesis

An illustrator in Dhaka spends three weeks on a commission. Four months later a friend sends her a screenshot: her work, mirrored and recoloured, on a product listing in another country. She has no registration, no timestamp, no leverage, and no lawyer. She posts about it. Nothing happens.

On the other side of that transaction is a brand manager who never intended to steal anything. An agency sent the asset. The agency got it from a freelancer. The freelancer found it. Nobody in that chain checked, because there was no practical way to check — and now the brand is in a dispute it cannot document its way out of, because nobody kept a record of what was verified before publication.

Both of those people are our users, and neither of them is the villain. The villain is a supply chain that moves images faster than it can account for them.

1.1 The insight

The market is selling verdicts. The market needs records.

Every incumbent in this space returns a number: 87% AI, 93% similar, match found. A number is an opinion with a decimal point. It cannot be audited, it cannot be handed to a counterparty, and it evaporates the moment someone asks "on what basis?" What organisations actually need before publishing is not a stronger opinion. It is a structured, timestamped, independently verifiable record of what was checked, what was found, and what was decided — the thing every other regulated industry already has and this one does not.

Financial services has the audit trail. Pharmaceuticals has the batch record. Food has the chain of custody. Creative publishing — an industry that moves hundreds of billions of dollars of commercial value through images — has a Slack thread and a hope.

1.2 Why this becomes infrastructure rather than a tool

Tools get replaced when a better tool appears. Infrastructure gets embedded and outlives its replacements, because the cost of removing it is not the price of the alternative — it is the cost of invalidating everything built on top of it. Three properties move CreatorProof from the first category to the second:

  • The record accumulates. A customer's evidence history is only continuous if it stays with one issuer and one transparency log. Twelve months in, switching means starting the audit trail again.
  • The catalog compounds. Every registered work makes every future scan more valuable to everyone, which is a supply-side network effect that a competitor cannot buy.
  • Co-attestation makes it multilateral. The moment two organisations co-sign a decision on chain, neither can unilaterally leave the standard. This is how a vendor becomes a network.

2.2 The Bangladesh wedge

The best first market for this company is the one it is being built in

Bangladesh is consistently ranked among the world's top two suppliers of online freelance labour, and a large share of that supply is creative and design work delivered to clients in the United States, the United Kingdom and the European Union. Those clients are precisely the ones whose contracts have started to require AI-disclosure warranties and originality representations.

That creates a very specific, very local, very urgent problem: a Bangladeshi studio can lose a Western contract because it cannot produce evidence, not because it did anything wrong. CreatorProof turns that from an existential risk into a competitive advantage — a studio that attaches a verifiable evidence packet to every delivery is easier to buy from than one that attaches a promise.

Strategically this is the ideal wedge: a domestic market we can reach without a sales team, solving a problem created by international buyers, which means every customer we win locally carries our artefact into a global enterprise. The proof packet is the marketing.

4.2 Why this shape

  • The platform fee buys predictability for us and budget-line simplicity for them, and it is what makes the audit trail continuous — the thing that creates switching cost.
  • The catalog fee is the network fee. It is the only charge that scales with the asset that makes the whole system more valuable to every other customer, which is why it is priced gently and never metered aggressively.
  • Scan metering aligns cost with value. A customer who scans more is publishing more, is earning more, and is exposed to more risk. Their bill should move with all three.
  • Forensic tier is a margin instrument. It costs us GPU seconds and it is the tier customers reach for when something is actually at stake, which is exactly when price sensitivity collapses.

6.3 The shadow pilot, in detail

The single most important sales mechanic we have, because it removes the buyer's real objection — which is never price, it is "what if it is wrong?"

  • Week 1 — Fit. Define the customer's policy in their own words, register a starter catalog, agree what a false positive would cost them.
  • Weeks 2–3 — Shadow. Every asset goes through CreatorProof and their existing process. We record both. We change nothing.
  • Week 4 — Review. We present disagreements, not agreements: where we flagged something they missed, and where we flagged something they were right to ignore. Showing a prospect where we were wrong is not a weakness in this motion, it is the mechanism — a vendor who reports their own false positives is a vendor whose true positives can be believed.
  • Decision. They have measured our value on their own data. The commercial conversation is now arithmetic instead of persuasion.
07

Defensibility

The honest question an investor asks is "what stops a well-funded incumbent from shipping this in a quarter?" Here is the answer, ordered from weakest to strongest.

Weak — the models

Our models are public research artefacts. Anyone can download them. We claim no moat here whatsoever, and any pitch that did would be lying. The models are a commodity input; the architecture around them is not.

Moderate — the architecture

Lane separation, coverage-before-verdict, nominate-then-verify, evidence-family governance and the abstention logic represent a substantial body of design judgement that took us the length of this build to get right. Copyable, but not quickly, and not by a team that has not already made our mistakes.

Strong — calibration data

Operating points calibrated against real customer corpora. Every pilot improves thresholds for the domain it came from. A competitor can match our models on day one and still be worse on an agency's actual assets, because they have not seen an agency's actual assets.

Strong — the catalog

A supply-side data network effect. Every registered work makes every scan more valuable to every customer. This is the asset that cannot be bought, only accumulated, and the reason registration is free.

Strongest — audit continuity

A customer's evidence history is bound to our issuer key and transparency log. Leaving does not cost them a migration; it costs them the continuity of their own audit trail. This is the same lock-in that keeps companies with their auditor.

Strongest — the co-attestation network

Once two organisations have jointly signed decisions on chain, the standard is no longer ours to control or theirs to abandon. Each additional member raises the cost of every member leaving. This is a genuine multilateral network effect, and it is already built.

09

Delivery playbook

How to present this so a judging panel feels it rather than files it. Rhetoric is not decoration — it is the difference between a project that is understood and a project that is remembered.

9.1 The narrative arc

Do not open with the technology. Open with a person. Judges evaluate dozens of projects that all begin "our solution leverages" and they blur into one. A specific human in a specific situation does not blur.

BeatEmotional targetHow to hit it
1 · The personRecognitionOne named illustrator, one stolen piece, one screenshot. Concrete, small, undeniable. Never "artists everywhere".
2 · The turnDiscomfortReveal that the person who used her work is also not a villain. Nobody checked because checking was impossible. The audience loses their easy target.
3 · The reframeCuriosity"This is not one question. It is three." State them. This is the moment the panel realises they had been thinking about it wrongly.
4 · The revealReliefShow the product doing it. Real screen, real image, real result. Let the interface speak; narrate lightly.
5 · The proofTrustOpen the receipt. This is where a demo becomes a company — the artefact outlives the demo.
6 · The horizonAmbitionOne sentence about the network, not five. Leave them extrapolating rather than listening.

9.2 Six techniques that will do most of the work

  • Specificity as a credibility instrument. "Three weeks of work, mirrored and recoloured, on a listing in another country" outperforms "widespread infringement" every time. Concrete detail signals lived understanding; abstraction signals a slide deck.
  • Restraint as proof of rigour. The most persuasive sentence available to us is "we could have put ninety-nine percent on this slide, and we did not, because we have not earned it." Voluntarily declining an advantage is the strongest honesty signal a presenter can send, and it makes everything else you say more believable.
  • Loss framing over gain framing. People are reliably more moved by what they stand to lose than by what they might gain. "The account you lose when you cannot answer the question" lands harder than "improved compliance posture".
  • The tricolon. Three-part structures are disproportionately memorable. Ours is already built in: what matched, what was checked, what was decided. Use it verbatim, more than once, so it becomes the phrase they repeat to each other afterwards.
  • The deliberate pause. After the reframe — "it is three questions" — stop for a full two seconds. Silence marks importance more reliably than emphasis does, and almost nobody uses it.
  • Peak-end weighting. Audiences remember the emotional peak and the final moment, not the average. Engineer the peak at the live match reveal and spend real effort on the last sentence, because those two moments are the memory.

9.3 Vocal delivery

PaceAround 150 words per minute. Slower than feels natural. Urgency comes from what you say, not how fast.
Terminal pitchLet pitch fall at the end of sentences. Rising terminals read as seeking approval; falling terminals read as stating fact.
VolumeDrop volume for the most important line rather than raising it. A panel leans in for a quiet sentence and defends against a loud one.
The demoNarrate less than you want to. Let two seconds pass after the match appears so the panel discovers it rather than being told.
The closeOne sentence, then stop. The last thing they hear should be your line, not your throat-clearing.

9.4 Pre-empting the four hard questions

If they askAnswer with
"How accurate is it, really?" The number, the method and the failure in one breath: "98.1% match recall over 364 transformed cases, zero false matches on 115 negatives, on a benchmark we built ourselves and will publish. Two cases fail, both flat repetitive graphics." Volunteering the failure is what makes the headline credible.
"Isn't the blockchain part unnecessary?" "It is unnecessary right up until the two parties disagree." A database timestamp is our word. An attestation is a witness that neither party controls, and co-attestation is two parties binding themselves to the same record. That is not decoration; it is the only part that survives a dispute.
"Why can't Google or Adobe do this tomorrow?" They can build the detection. They will not want the liability of anchoring a rights decision and co-signing it with a counterparty. The defensible position is the one their legal department declines.
"Who actually pays?" The party with publication risk, never the creator. Name the buyer, the budget line and the signature: head of production, existing risk budget, one signature at Studio tier.

9.5 The closing line

Say this, then stop

"We cannot tell you who owns an image. Nobody can. What we can do is make sure that the next time somebody has to answer for one, there is a record — and that the record was not written by the person who benefits from it."
