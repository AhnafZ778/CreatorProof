How you’d explain the AIto a person.

Read this out loud. If a sentence sounds like a datasheet, skip it. These are the words you’d use with a smart friend who has never trained a model.

The dark architecture pack is for pointing. This is for your mouth.

01Start with a person, not a model

The scene

A brand is about to post a picture. Legal walks over and asks: is this safe? That sounds like one question. It isn’t. It’s three different questions, and they need three different answers — because the next move is different for each one.

If they already licensed the photo, that’s paperwork. If a generator probably made it, that’s a disclosure problem. If it just feels like someone’s drawing style, that’s a conversation, not a lawsuit. Smash those into one percentage and nobody can act. So we don’t.

Copy

Is this the same picture as one we already have on file — and which bit of it?

Origin

Did a generator leave fingerprints, or a signed note, that it helped make this?

Style

Does this look a lot like a creator who asked us to watch? Looking like them is not stealing.

Say this

Everyone else hands you a percentage. A percentage can’t tell you which question failed. We answer all three, separately, and we also tell you if we never actually looked.

02What happens when someone uses the site

They open the website, drop in a picture, hit scan. The page doesn’t sit there grinding. It says “got it” almost immediately, and the slow work happens in the back. Think restaurant: the waiter takes the order in ten seconds. The kitchen takes a couple of minutes. If the fryer dies, the dining room doesn’t close — the tickets just wait.

When it’s done you don’t get a vibe. You get three cards: was AI used, does it reuse a work, does it resemble a creator. You can open the microscope and see the actual overlapping patch, with little matching points drawn on. Then a rulebook the customer wrote — not the model — says pass, send to a human, or block. After that we freeze the answer so nobody can quietly edit it later. That’s the blockchain half.

03The trick all the neural nets share

Forget ChatGPT. Nothing here writes a paragraph. Every picture gets squeezed into a long list of numbers — like a fingerprint, or the GPS coordinates of a song. Pictures the model thinks are “the same kind of thing” land near each other. Distance is a ranking, not a percentage of guilt. Nobody should ever say “this is 87% stolen.”

Why not one giant brain that just tells you the answer? Because that brain says it slightly differently every time you ask. You can’t stamp a sentence that won’t sit still. So we use small specialist fingerprints, each trained for one job, each returning the same kind of answer if you run it twice.

When they say “is that a transformer?”

Yeah. A vision transformer chops the picture into a grid of little tiles and lets every tile look at every other tile. Fancy name, simple idea: the model isn’t scanning left to right like a person reading. It’s asking “how do all the bits relate.” We use two different ones. They do not share a brain. One is for “same photo?”, one is for “same drawing style?”, one smaller one is for “does this look generated?”

04Copy, part one — the librarian (SSCD)

First job: rummage the catalog really fast. The model is called SSCD. Ugly name. What it was trained to do is the whole point: not “this is a cat.” It was trained on pictures that had been cropped, jpeg’d, recolored, slapped with a logo — and it still had to say “that’s the same photo.” That’s what an infringer actually does. So it’s good at finding abused copies, and bad at being a general art critic. Fine. That’s the job.

It turns the picture into about five hundred numbers, and also looks at the middle and the four corners, because a stolen logo usually lives in a corner, not the whole poster. Then it picks the eight closest things in the catalog. Eight suspects. That’s it. The librarian is not allowed to accuse anyone. Librarians are fast and a bit sloppy. We like them for fetching. We do not let them testify.

If they say “so it’s reverse image search”

Google looks at the whole internet and says “similar vibes.” We look at the pictures this company is actually allowed to use, and then we still have to prove it the old-fashioned way. Similar is not copied.

05Copy, part two — the lineup (this is not AI)

Here’s the part that makes the copy answer believable. We stop using the neural net. We do what computers did in 2004. We hunt for little landmarks in both pictures — a window corner, a speck of paint, a zip on a jacket — a couple of thousand of them. Then we ask: do a bunch of those landmarks line up under one honest warp? Cropped, rotated, resized, even flipped in a mirror — still one warp. If they don’t line up, it’s not a copy. Two photos of the same beach at sunset will “feel” similar to the librarian and then fail this test, which is exactly what you want.

We need at least a dozen landmarks agreeing, spread around, not all hiding in one corner. If that passes, we actually bend one picture onto the other and ask: in the overlap, do the pixels agree, or did we just get unlucky with a few corners? Both have to say yes. Same file down to the last byte is the only shortcut. Everything else has to survive the lineup and the pixel check.

Say this

You can trick the librarian with a weird noise pattern. You cannot invent twelve landmarks that all line up with a picture you never copied. That’s why the neural net fetches, and the old geometry accuses.

06Origin — a signed note beats a vibe detector

Everyone knows “AI detectors” are messy. So we don’t start there. Best evidence is a signed note stuffed inside the file by the camera or the generator — C2PA, same idea as a wax seal on a letter. If the seal checks out, we’re done arguing. If there’s no seal, that doesn’t mean a human made it. It just means nobody signed it. A lot of real photos have no seal. A lot of fake ones don’t either.

Next we literally read the picture, including the four corners, for printed text like “AI generated.” That’s where watermarks sit, and where people crop them off. No text means nothing. Text means a human should look.

Only then the vibe detectors. We run more than one, from different families, and they have to agree. If they argue, we say we don’t know — we do not pick a winner. And by default this whole lane is advice. It cannot block a post on its own. If a customer wants it to be a gate, they turn that on. You’re not going to stand on stage and pretend detectors are magic. You’re going to say we treated them as the weakest evidence, on purpose.

07Style — handwriting, not the words (CSD)

Style is not copyright. We say that first, every time, or a judge will eat you. This lane exists because a creator whose look is getting cloned at scale still deserves a heads-up.

Different fingerprint. About seven hundred numbers, trained to care about how it was made and ignore what it depicts. Same illustrator, two different cats: should be close. Two photographs of the same building, different photographers: should not. That’s CSD. Think handwriting, not the sentence.

We don’t score against a random person. The creator has to opt in, with at least a few of their works, before we even run the numbers. If they never confirmed, or they revoked, we produce nothing. And some styles sit in the middle of the room and look a bit like everyone — that’s a known trap — so we knock those “popular” profiles down instead of letting them win every comparison. We also check the copy fingerprint on the side: if it’s the same building, that’s content, not style. We don’t want to confuse those.

Ceiling of this lane, said plainly: we can ask a human to look. We cannot say they stole it. We cannot change the copy answer. If you oversell this in the room, you deserve the follow-up.

Say this

Copy is “same photo.” Style is “same handwriting.” Handwriting isn’t illegal. We only look if the writer asked us to, and the loudest thing we can do is tap someone on the shoulder.

08The models don’t get to be the boss

This is the bit most products skip, and it’s why theirs quietly go feral. The models report what they saw. They cannot see the customer’s licences. They cannot press the block button. A written rulebook the customer owns — a document, not a vibe — turns those findings into pass, review, or block. When they upgrade a model next year, the rulebook doesn’t secretly rewrite itself. You can replay last March’s scan against today’s rules and see what would change. That’s the difference between a detector and something a lawyer can live with.

Same honesty on failure. If the detector times out, we do not draw a green tick. “We couldn’t look” is a different sentence from “we looked and found nothing.” A green tick you didn’t earn is how people get sued.
