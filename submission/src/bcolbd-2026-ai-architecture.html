Opening line

A brand is about to publish an image and asks one question: is this safe? Every product on the market answers with a single percentage, and a percentage is not something a legal team can act on.

So we split it into three questions that have different evidence, different failure modes, and different consequences.

A1

Three questions, not one

The core argument

“Is this safe to publish?” collapses three unrelated questions with three different kinds of evidence. Averaging them into one score destroys the only information a reviewer can act on — which question failed, and why.

  1. 1Genuinely independent. Each lane uses different models on different evidence, so a failure in one cannot silently corrupt another.
  2. 2Answers are specific. Not “87% risky” but which work, which region, how many verified matching points.
  3. 3Averaging destroys meaning. A blended score cannot distinguish “clear copy” from “vaguely similar style”, yet those need opposite actions.
  4. 4Separation is the product. Three answers plus coverage is what makes the output usable by a legal team rather than merely interesting.
Say this

Every competitor returns one number. But a copy of a photo you already licensed and a picture that merely resembles someone’s style require completely opposite actions.

One score cannot tell those apart, so we refuse to produce one.

If they push back

“Isn’t this just reverse image search?” Reverse search finds similar pictures on the open web. We search a declared rights-bearing catalogue and then geometrically prove which region of which work was reused.

“Why not one big vision model?” A general model gives a plausible-sounding sentence. We need a reproducible, hash-stable output, because the result gets signed and anchored — a model that phrases things differently on a re-run cannot be evidence.

A2

Serving topology

How inference is actually deployed

Inference is asynchronous and horizontally scaled: the API accepts a scan and returns immediately, and GPU workers pull from a durable queue. Nothing about a request is blocked on a model that takes two seconds.

  1. 1Credentials never reach the browser. The Next.js layer proxies server-side, so no API key is exposed to a client device.
  2. 2A durable queue, not a thread. A worker crash mid-scan replays the job rather than losing the customer’s request.
  3. 3Only workers need GPUs. The expensive tier scales independently, which is what keeps unit cost predictable.
  4. 4Latency is a product decision. Returning in milliseconds and completing in seconds keeps the integration simple for the customer.
Say this

The architecture assumes models are slow and occasionally unavailable. Everything expensive sits behind a durable queue, so the worst a model failure can do is slow a queue down — never take the service off the air.

A3

The inference pipeline, stage by stage

End-to-end flow

Eight stages, each of which reports its own outcome and its own coverage. These are the exact stage names the API streams to the client, so what the judge sees in the demo matches this diagram label for label.

  1. 1Provenance outranks statistics. A valid C2PA manifest is hard evidence and is trusted above any statistical detector.
  2. 2Recall then verify. A cheap vector search proposes candidates; expensive geometry only runs on the shortlist.
  3. 3Verification is per candidate. Each shortlisted work is independently proven or rejected, so one strong match cannot drag others along.
  4. 4Coverage travels with the result. If a stage could not run, that fact is recorded and surfaced rather than quietly ignored.
Say this

Watch the stage names during the demo — they are the same eight you see here. Each one records not just what it found but whether it was able to look at all, and that second field is what makes the first one trustworthy.

A4

Copy lane — recall, then proof

Lane 1 · same work reused

A neural embedding is fast but only ever suggests. Nothing becomes a match until classical geometry proves a pixel-level correspondence and an aligned structural comparison agrees — so an embedding that is merely confused cannot accuse anyone.

  1. 1Crops are searched too. A logo lifted into the corner of a banner is found because five regional views are queried alongside the whole frame.
  2. 2Geometry is not a score. It either fits a consistent transform between the two images or it does not, and we store the matching point pairs as evidence.
  3. 3Compare after aligning. Measuring structure on un-aligned images is meaningless; we warp first, then measure only the overlapping area.
  4. 4Two independent confirmations. Geometry and structure use unrelated mathematics, so both agreeing is what earns the word “match”.
Say this

The embedding is a librarian — it fetches eight books that might be the one. The geometry is the proof: it finds hundreds of specific points that line up under a single consistent transform.

Only the second one is allowed to say the word “match”.

If they push back

“Embeddings can be fooled by adversarial noise.” Which is exactly why they cannot decide. An adversarial vector might get a work into the shortlist, but it cannot manufacture 12 geometrically consistent keypoint correspondences with a work it did not copy.

“What about crops, rotations and re-encodes?” The homography absorbs crop, scale, rotation and mirror; JPEG re-encoding barely moves SSCD vectors. Our transform benchmark covers 26 of these and is on sheet A13.

A5

Origin lane — a ladder, not a verdict

Lane 2 · was a model involved

AI detection is the least reliable claim in this space, so we rank the evidence by how much it deserves to be trusted. Cryptographic provenance beats a declared label, which beats a statistical detector — and the detector tier is advisory by default.

  1. 1Provenance is decisive. A validated manifest is a signed statement from the tool that made the file, so it outranks every statistical guess.
  2. 2Labels are read, not assumed. We OCR the corners specifically, because that is where generators put their marks and where crops remove them.
  3. 3One detector is never enough. Two independent model families must agree, and each must be calibrated on held-out data before its number is used at all.
  4. 4Abstention is a first-class result. Low resolution, disagreement or instability under re-crop each produce a named inconclusive state rather than a coin flip.
Say this

Everyone in this room knows AI detectors are unreliable. So we treat them as the weakest tier of a ladder, require two independent families to agree, and by default the whole lane is advisory — it cannot block a publication on its own.

If they push back

“AI detectors have terrible false-positive rates.” Agreed, which is why this lane cannot change the copy verdict and defaults to informational. It reports evidence; a customer who wants it to gate publication must opt in explicitly.

“C2PA is barely adopted.” True today and improving fast — Adobe, Leica, OpenAI and Google all emit it. Our architecture is built so that as adoption rises, the hard tier absorbs cases the statistical tier currently handles.

A6

Creator-profile lane — resemblance, stated as resemblance

Lane 3 · style

Style is not copyrightable, and we never say it is. This lane exists because a creator whose body of work is being imitated at scale deserves to know — so it produces a consent-gated resemblance reading that can request review but can never assert infringement.

  1. 1Style, not subject. CSD descriptors are trained specifically to encode how something was made rather than what it depicts.
  2. 2Hub correction matters. CSLS discounts profiles that sit near everything, which is the classic failure of raw cosine ranking on style vectors.
  3. 3Opt-in by construction. A profile with unconfirmed or revoked consent produces no reading at all — the gate runs before the model, not after.
  4. 4A deliberate legal ceiling. The most this lane can ever do is ask a human to look, which keeps the product on the right side of a genuinely unsettled area of law.
Say this

Style is not protected by copyright anywhere, and we did not build a product that pretends otherwise. What we built is a way for a creator who opted in to find out that something closely resembling their work is circulating — and then decide for themselves what to do.

A7

The firewall between detection and decision

The most important design choice

Models never decide anything. They produce evidence; a separately versioned policy document turns evidence into an action. That separation is why a model upgrade cannot silently change what a customer’s pipeline blocks.

  1. 1Models are blind to consequences. No model can see a licence or a threshold, so no model can be tuned into a business outcome.
  2. 2Only evidence crosses. The firewall carries findings and coverage — never a suggested action.
  3. 3Policy is a document. It is versioned JSON the customer owns, so a compliance officer can read and diff it without reading Python.
  4. 4Two systems, two audits. “Was the detection correct?” and “was the rule correct?” become separate questions with separate answers.
Say this

This is the line most products get wrong. They tune the model until it blocks the right things, which means every model update silently rewrites the company’s policy.

Ours cannot, because the model has never been allowed to see the policy.

If they push back

“Isn’t that just extra layers?” It is the difference between a defensible and an indefensible audit. When a regulator asks why an image was blocked, we point at a policy version with a date; when they ask whether the evidence was sound, we point at the packet. Fusing them makes both questions unanswerable.

“Who sets the thresholds?” The customer, per workspace. A news wire and an advertising agency need genuinely different lines, and neither should have to ask us to retrain something.

A8

Coverage — the difference between “clean” and “unchecked”

Honest failure

A system that returns “no match” when it never actually searched is worse than no system, because it manufactures false confidence. Every scan reports what it was able to examine, and an incomplete search is a distinct outcome with its own name.

  1. 1A clean result is a claim. It asserts that the declared catalogue was fully searched, and that assertion is checkable.
  2. 2Degradation is visible. If one reference image failed to embed, coverage drops and the scan says so rather than quietly skipping it.
  3. 3Empty is not clean. Scanning against nothing returns a distinct state, so an unconfigured workspace cannot look like a passing one.
  4. 4Per-capability truth. Each model records whether it ran, was skipped, or was unavailable, which is what makes the packet auditable later.
Say this

Ask any competing tool what happens when its detector times out. It returns a pass. Ours returns SCOPE_INCOMPLETE with the reason code, because the only thing more dangerous than a wrong answer is a confident one.

A9

Retrieval at scale

From one catalogue to ten million works

The expensive part of this system is comparing an image against every work that could have been copied. We make that cheap with a three-tier funnel: exact identity first, then approximate vector recall, and only then the heavy geometric proof.

  1. 1Identity before intelligence. Most real reuse is an unmodified re-upload, and a hash lookup answers that in milliseconds.
  2. 2Recall is sub-linear. A graph index means doubling the catalogue does not double the search time.
  3. 3The expensive stage is bounded. Geometry always runs on about eight images, so the slowest part of the pipeline has constant cost.
  4. 4Cost per scan is flat. That is what makes per-scan pricing viable rather than a bespoke enterprise deal every time.
Say this

The naive version of this product compares one image against a million images and dies. We only ever run the expensive proof on about eight candidates, so the cost of a scan barely moves as the catalogue grows from a thousand works to ten million.

A10

Model Lab — how a model earns the right to run

MLOps and reproducibility

No model reaches production because someone liked its demo. Each one climbs a six-rung qualification ladder with a written gate at every rung, and the exact bundle of versions that judged a scan is recorded inside that scan.

  1. 1Most models never reach the top. A model can be useful at rung four and still be barred from influencing a decision.
  2. 2Gates use confidence bounds. We promote on the lower bound of a Wilson interval, so a small favourable sample cannot pass a model.
  3. 3Reproducibility is a stored field. Given a scan id we can reconstruct the exact model set that produced it, which is what makes the evidence hold up.
  4. 4Demotion is automatic. Drift against the baseline pulls a model back down the ladder without waiting for a human to notice.
Say this

Every team here can show a model that works. The question is what happens on the four-hundredth day, when the weights have been swapped twice and someone disputes a result from last March. We can answer that, because the scan carries the bundle that judged it.

A11

Evidence Microscope — showing the work

Explainability the user can act on

The reviewer sees the actual evidence, not a bar chart of a score. Six inspection modes, one per question, each drawing on the same signed packet the API returned — so what is on screen and what is in the record cannot diverge.

  1. 1Evidence, not attribution heat. We draw the geometrically verified correspondences, which are checkable, rather than a saliency blur that is not.
  2. 2Style gets its own view. A tile map shows resemblance is diffuse across the frame, which is precisely why it is not a copy.
  3. 3Portable coordinates. Normalised geometry means a customer can rebuild the same overlay in their own tooling from the packet alone.
  4. 4Read-only by design. The UI has no path to modify a verdict, so what a reviewer sees is what was recorded.
Say this

Explainability in most AI products means a percentage with a colour. Ours means the reviewer sees the two images aligned, the matched region outlined, and the specific points that line up — and can disagree with us on the evidence rather than on faith.

A12

Responsible AI — the risks we designed against

Risks & challenges

A system that accuses people of copying has real failure costs. These are the five ways it could cause harm and the structural control that makes each one hard to reach — controls in the architecture, not promises in a policy document.

Risk What it would look like The structural control
False accusation of copying An embedding finds two visually similar but independent works and the system calls it a match. A match is impossible without a validated homography and aligned structural agreement. Similarity alone is capped at INCONCLUSIVE, and both confirmations use unrelated mathematics.
AI-detector false positives on human work A heavily edited photograph is labelled machine-generated and the photographer is penalised. The origin lane is advisory by default and cannot change the copy verdict. Two independent, individually calibrated families must agree, and disagreement produces a named inconclusive state.
Style policing An artist is blocked for painting in a genre that resembles someone with a profile on the platform. Consent is checked before scoring; the lane can only request human review; the word infringement never appears in a style finding. A profile needs at least three works before it exists at all.
Silent degradation A model is unavailable, the scan returns clean, and a customer publishes believing they were checked. Coverage and per-capability execution state are mandatory fields on every scan. An unrun stage produces SCOPE_INCOMPLETE, never a pass.
Creator data misuse Registered works or creator profiles leak, or are used to train something the creator never agreed to. Works stay in the customer’s own scope, enforced at the index and again by row-level security. Only 32-byte hashes ever reach the public chain, and nothing registered is used as training data.
Say this

Notice that none of these controls are “we will be careful”. Each one is a place in the code where the harmful outcome is structurally unreachable — a match cannot exist without geometry, a style score cannot exist without consent.

If they push back

“What about bias across cultures or art traditions?” The copy lane is geometric, so it is invariant to subject matter by construction. The style lane is the one with real exposure, which is exactly why it is consent-gated, advisory only, and gated behind a per-profile minimum sample count.

“Who is accountable when it is wrong?” The customer’s policy made the decision and the packet shows the evidence it was based on. Both are versioned and both are anchored, so an incorrect outcome is investigable rather than deniable.

Measured performance

Evidence for the claims

Every number below comes from a harness in the repository that a judge can run. The benchmark is built around transformations an infringer actually applies — crops, rotations, mirrors, re-encodes and overlays — not a clean academic split.

99.45%returned match or review — only two cases missed outright

What the positive set contains

Transformation familyBehaviour
Crop, 60–95% of frameRecovered by regional query views and absorbed by the homography.
Rotation, mirror, transposeMirror was a known miss at 0 of 14; the mirrored-descriptor retry now recovers 14 of 14.
JPEG re-encode, resize, blurSSCD vectors move very little; geometry is unaffected.
Overlays, borders, colour shiftsMasked structural comparison ignores the added region rather than being dragged down by it.
Remaining failuresTwo flat repetitive graphics with almost no distinctive keypoints — reported honestly as inconclusive rather than guessed.

How the numbers are computed

  • Wilson intervals, not point estimates. Every rate is reported with a confidence interval, and promotion gates read the lower bound.
  • Lineage-clustered bootstrap. Twenty-six transformations of one source image are not twenty-six independent samples, so resampling happens at the source-work level.
  • Negatives are hard. The negative set is visually similar but independent work, which is the case that actually breaks embedding-only systems.
  • Scores are labelled non-probabilistic. The evidence index is an ordering, and the packet says so explicitly rather than dressing it up as a percentage.
Say this

The number I care about is the zero. Recall can be bought by lowering a threshold; zero false matches on hard negatives is what makes the output usable by a legal team.

A14

Where the AI output becomes evidence

Hand-off to the proof layer

An AI verdict that can be regenerated differently tomorrow is an opinion. We make the output deterministic, sign it, and anchor its hash publicly — which is what converts a model result into something a court can date.

  1. 1Fixed vocabulary, not prose. Enums and numbers serialise identically every time; a generated sentence does not.
  2. 2The digest covers the reasoning. Model versions and policy version are inside the hashed packet, so nobody can later claim a different configuration was used.
  3. 3Only 32 bytes go public. The image, the catalogue and the customer’s identity never cross the boundary.
  4. 4Determinism is the constraint that shaped the AI. It is why we did not build this on a large multimodal model that writes an explanation.
Say this

This is why the two submissions are one system. The AI decides what is true about the image; the chain makes it impossible for anyone — including us — to change that answer afterwards.

A15

Q&A armory — the hard technical questions

Part one of two

Each answer is two or three sentences, followed by the sheet to turn to. Answer, then point — do not read the sheet aloud.

Question 01Why not just use one large multimodal model?

Because the output has to be byte-identical on a re-run, or it cannot be hashed, signed or anchored. A general model writes a plausible sentence that changes between runs, which is interesting but is not evidence. Turn to A14 · the determinism panel

Question 02AI detectors are famously unreliable. Why trust yours?

We do not, and the architecture says so. Origin detection is the weakest tier of a three-tier ladder, requires two independently calibrated model families to agree, and is advisory by default — it cannot block anything on its own. Turn to A5 · the ladder, bottom tier

Question 03Isn’t this reverse image search with extra steps?

Reverse search finds similar pictures on the open web and stops there. We search a declared rights-bearing catalogue, then geometrically prove which region of which work was reused, and return the matching point pairs as evidence. Turn to A4 · steps two and three

Question 04These are all off-the-shelf models. Where is the novelty?

The novelty is not a new backbone, it is the evidence contract around them: three separated lanes, mandatory coverage reporting, a firewall between detection and policy, and a deterministic packet that can be anchored. Nobody else outputs something a lawyer can use. Turn to A7 · then A8

Question 05What stops an adversarial image fooling you?

An adversarial perturbation can push an embedding around, so at worst it gets a work into or out of an eight-item shortlist. It cannot fabricate twelve geometrically consistent keypoint correspondences with an image it never copied. Turn to A4 · callout two

Question 06What did you train, and on whose data?

We train no backbone on customer material — registered works are never training data. What we fit is calibration: a two-parameter mapping learned on held-out labelled data, plus per-profile prototypes built only from works a creator consented to enrol. Turn to A10 · rung five, and A6 · the consent gate

Question 07What is the latency, and can it run in a real workflow?

The API accepts in under a fifth of a second and the full scan completes in about two and a half seconds, because the heavy geometry only ever runs on eight candidates regardless of catalogue size. Turn to A9 · the funnel, then A2

Question 08What happens when a new generator appears next month?

A new detector is added as an additional evidence family and enters at the bottom of the qualification ladder; it cannot influence a result until it is calibrated and passes the written gate. Meanwhile the copy and profile lanes are entirely unaffected. Turn to A10 · the ladder and the gate

A16

Q&A armory — risk, ethics and business

Part two of two

If a question is not here, answer it with the same shape: name the risk, then point to where in the architecture it is made unreachable.

Question 09Detecting artistic style is legally dangerous. Aren’t you exposed?

Style is not copyrightable and we never claim it is. The lane is opt-in per creator, produces a resemblance reading rather than a finding, and its maximum possible effect is to ask a human to take a look. Turn to A6 · the boundary panel

Question 10Prove the accuracy. Numbers, not adjectives.

Three hundred and sixty-four positives across twenty-six real transformation families: 98.1% returned a match, 99.45% returned match or review, and zero of one hundred and fifteen hard negatives produced a false match. Turn to A13 · the four figures across the top

Question 11What if the model is wrong and someone is falsely accused?

A match requires two independent confirmations using unrelated mathematics, and even then the system only ever produces evidence — the customer’s own policy decides the action. Every claim comes with the region outlined so it can be disputed on the evidence. Turn to A12 · row one, then A11

Question 12Why does an AI product need blockchain at all?

Because the value of a pre-publication check is the date on it, and we are the party being paid to produce that date. Anchoring the finding’s hash means our customer’s defence does not depend on trusting us. Turn to A14 · then the blockchain pack, B1

Question 13How do you avoid bias against particular art traditions?

The copy lane is geometric, so it is invariant to subject and culture by construction. The style lane is the one with genuine exposure, which is why it is consent-gated, advisory only, and requires a minimum sample count before a profile can be scored. Turn to A12 · row three

Question 14Do you keep customer images? What about privacy?

Media stays inside the customer’s own scope, enforced at the retrieval index and again by row-level security in the database. Only a 32-byte hash is ever published, so nothing about the image or the customer crosses the public boundary. Turn to A9 · the footer, and A14 · step five

Question 15Couldn’t Adobe or Google ship this next quarter?

They can ship detection; they cannot ship neutrality. A platform that also hosts the content benefits from the answer, and a finding only counts if its author does not. Turn to A7 · the firewall, then B2

Question 16Who actually pays for this, and why?

Agencies, publishers and marketplaces absorbing takedown risk today with no record of having checked. They are buying a dated, signed record of diligence, not detection. Turn to A1 · then A14

Closing line for the session

Most teams built a detector. We built the system a detector is one component of: it records what it checked, what it could not, who decided and when — then makes all four impossible to quietly revise.
